    How it works

    1. While you talk, the browser's speech recognizer writes the transcript and a MediaRecorder keeps the raw audio. Both are stored in this browser's IndexedDB. The page uploads nothing, though Chrome's recognizer may process speech on Google's servers.
    2. Each clip is resampled to 16 kHz, trimmed of silence, loudness-matched and turned into a log-magnitude spectrogram: one frame every 10 ms, 257 frequency bins each.
    3. The acoustic network reads one letter at a time, plus three letters either side and the position inside the letter, and predicts one spectrogram frame. A second small network learns how many frames each letter lasts.
    4. Nobody tells it where each letter sits in the audio. It starts by spreading letters evenly across the clip. After 30 epochs it re-aligns every clip with monotonic alignment search, using its own predictions, and keeps refining that as it trains. Pick a clip below to watch the alignment move.
    5. To speak, it predicts durations and spectrogram frames for your text, and the Griffin-Lim algorithm estimates the missing phase to turn the spectrogram back into a waveform.

    What to expect: with a few clips you get a hum in your tone and rhythm. After 20 or more clips and a few hundred epochs, syllables form. More varied sentences, a quiet room and the same microphone each time help the most. It's a tiny model trained on minutes of audio, so expect a mumbly, whispery version of you, not studio speech.
